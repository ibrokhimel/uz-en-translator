## English → Uzbek

### 1. Verb last (SOV), modifiers before the head
English keeps the verb in the middle; Uzbek puts it at the end, and everything that describes a noun comes before it.
- EN: *We opened a new office in Samarkand last week.*
- Bad: *Biz ochdik yangi ofis Samarqandda oʻtgan hafta.*
- Good: *Oʻtgan hafta Samarqandda yangi ofis ochdik.*

Typical order: time → place → object → verb. Put the new or important information right before the verb.

### 2. Relative clauses become participles
English *that / which / who* clauses become a participle placed **before** the noun.
- *the book that I read* → *men oʻqigan kitob*
- *people who work remotely* → *masofadan ishlaydigan odamlar*
- *the city where he was born* → *u tugʻilgan shahar*

Never translate *which* as *qaysiki* / *-ki* constructions; it sounds like a calque from Persian or Russian syntax and is old-fashioned.

### 3. No articles
*a / an / the* usually disappear. Use *bir* only when "one / a certain" carries meaning (*Bir kuni…* — "One day…").

### 4. Plural after numbers and quantity words
No *-lar* after a number or *koʻp, bir necha, har*:
- *three books* → *uchta kitob* (not *uchta kitoblar*)
- *many people* → *koʻp odam* / *koʻpchilik* (not *koʻp odamlar* in careful style)
- *bir necha*, *bir qancha*, *har* → singular: *bir necha kun*, *har bir xodim*.
- *koʻplab* is the exception: *koʻplab kompaniyalar* (with plural) is standard in news and web writing; *koʻplab kompaniya* reads stiff. *koʻpgina* works either way.
- *barcha*, *hamma* take the plural: *barcha xodimlar*.
- *koʻp* + plural (*koʻp talabalar*) is very common and not wrong; singular (*koʻp talaba*) is the more careful written norm. When translating, prefer the singular. When **reviewing** someone else's text, list it as a style suggestion, not an error.

**Verb agreement with plural subjects.** A 3rd-person plural subject does not need *-lar* on the verb: *Talabalar keldi* is neutral. *-lar* on the verb (*keldilar*) adds emphasis on the people or respect, and is required for honorific singular (*Dadam keldilar*). After a singular noun with *koʻp / bir necha* use the singular verb: *koʻp talaba qatnasha olmadi*. In reviews, *koʻp talabalar … olmadilar* is a style point, not an error.

### 5. Drop subject pronouns
The verb ending already marks the person. Keep the pronoun only for emphasis or contrast.
- *I think we should start.* → *Menimcha, boshlashimiz kerak.*

### 6. Possession
*Ali's book* → *Alining kitobi*; *the company's goal* → *kompaniyaning maqsadi*. Chains of possessives are fine but split if more than three.

### 7. Passive voice
English passive is often more natural as an active or impersonal sentence in Uzbek.
- *It was decided to postpone the meeting.* → *Uchrashuvni keyinga qoldirishga qaror qilindi.*
- *The report was written by experts.* → *Hisobotni mutaxassislar tayyorladi.*

### 8. Long sentences
English allows long chains with commas and *and*. Split them; Uzbek participle chains get heavy fast. One main idea per sentence, two clauses at most for web text.

### 9. Infinitives and nominalization
English *to + verb* often becomes *-ish* + case, or *uchun*:
- *to improve quality* → *sifatni yaxshilash uchun*
- *It is important to rest.* → *Dam olish muhim.*

### 10. "You" and politeness
Pick *siz* or *sen* once (see SKILL.md defaults) and keep verb endings consistent (*bilasiz* vs *bilasan*). Generic English "you" ("you can see that…") is often better as impersonal: *koʻrish mumkinki…*, *koʻrinib turibdiki…*.

### 11. Questions
Yes/no questions take *-mi* on the word being asked about: *Siz ertaga kelasizmi?* Do not translate "do/does".

### 12. Tense mapping (general guide)
| English | Uzbek |
|---|---|
| present simple (habit) | *-adi* (*ishlaydi*) |
| present continuous | *-yapti* (*ishlayapti*), literary *-moqda* |
| future | *-adi*, plans *-moqchi*, certainty *-ajak* (formal) |
| past simple | *-di* (*ishladi*) |
| present perfect / reported | *-gan* (*ishlagan*) |
| past continuous | *-ayotgan edi* |

For news style, *-moqda* reads more formal than *-yapti*.

### 13. Don't mark the reason (or purpose) twice
Use one marker per clause. English *because (of)* becomes either a clause with *chunki* or a postposition — not both.
- Bad: *…bekor qilindi, chunki ob-havo sababli.*
- Good: *Ob-havo sababli … bekor qilindi.* / *… bekor qilindi, chunki ob-havo yomon edi.*
Same for purpose: *…uchun* or *…maqsadida*, not *maqsadida … uchun*.

### 14. *qaysiki*, *-ki* relative clauses
These are calques of the English/Russian relative pronoun. Replace with a participle before the noun (see 2): *Uchrashuv qaysiki dushanbaga rejalashtirilgan edi* → *Dushanbaga rejalashtirilgan uchrashuv*.

## Uzbek → English

### 1. Restore subjects
Uzbek drops pronouns; English needs them. Use the verb ending to recover the person: *Ertaga kelaman* → *I'll come tomorrow.*

### 2. *u / ular* have no gender
Decide *he / she / it / they* from context. If context does not say, use *they* or rephrase with the name or role, and mention the choice in translator notes when it matters.

### 3. Honorific plural is not a plural
*Dadam keldilar*, *Ustoz aytdilar* — one person, shown respect. Translate as singular (*My father came*). Carry the respect with word choice if needed, not with grammar.

### 4. Evidential and inferential forms
These carry meaning English expresses with words:
- *-ibdi* (*kelibdi*) — reported or discovered: *apparently he came*, *it turns out he came*.
- *ekan* — realization or hearsay: *U shifokor ekan* → *So he's a doctor* / *Apparently he's a doctor*.
- *-mish* — hearsay, often skeptical: *They say…*, *supposedly…*.
Don't flatten these into plain past tense.

### 5. Participle chains
Unpack them into relative clauses or separate sentences: *Kecha biz koʻrgan film* → *the film we watched yesterday*.

### 6. Converbs and serial verbs
*-ib* chains link actions: *Uyga borib, ovqatlanib, uxladim* → *I went home, ate and went to sleep.* Auxiliary verbs add aspect, not separate actions: *yozib qoʻydi* (wrote down, for later), *oʻqib chiqdi* (read through), *aytib yubordi* (blurted out), *kelib qoldi* (turned up unexpectedly).

### 7. Politeness formulas
Translate function, not words:
- *Assalomu alaykum* → *Hello* / *Good morning* (formal letters: *Dear…*)
- *Hurmatli mijoz* → *Dear customer*
- *Rahmat, sogʻ boʻling* → *Thank you so much*
- *Xudo xohlasa / Inshoolloh* → keep or render as *hopefully*, depending on the audience.

### 8. Kinship and address terms
*aka, opa, uka, singil, amaki, togʻa, xola, amma, ustoz, domla* — English often lacks an exact word. Options: keep the name only, use a neutral equivalent (*brother*, *teacher*), or keep the Uzbek term with a short gloss on first use in cultural texts.

### 9. Word order back to SVO
Move the verb forward and the time/place to the end or start as English prefers: *Biz kecha Toshkentda uchrashdik* → *We met in Tashkent yesterday.*

### 10. Names with patronymics
*Aziz Karimovich* = first name + patronymic (father's name), the polite formal address — not a surname. In English keep it as is: *Dear Aziz Karimovich*. Never *Mr. Karimovich*. Use *Mr./Ms. + surname* only when the surname is given (*Karimov*). The same goes for *-ovna/-evna* (women) and Uzbek forms *Karim oʻgʻli*, *Karim qizi*.

### 11. Dates, deadlines and missing years
- English date style: follow the user's variety if the request or text shows it (US *December 15*). **Default: day before month, no comma — *15 December*.** It matches the Uzbek order, so it can't be misread, and it is standard in international English.
- *-gacha* with a deadline means "by" (the date is included): *10-dekabrgacha tasdiqlang* → *please confirm by 10 December*. Use "until" for duration (*kechgacha ishladi* → *worked until evening*).
- *soat 10:00 da* → *at 10:00* (or *10 a.m.*, matching the variety).
- Don't add a year the source doesn't give. If it matters, mention it in the notes instead.

### 12. Repetition and pairs
Uzbek uses paired words (*ota-ona* parents, *qarindosh-urugʻ* relatives, *mehr-oqibat* kindness and care) and reduplication (*sekin-sekin* slowly, *katta-katta* big ones). Translate as one natural English word or phrase.
